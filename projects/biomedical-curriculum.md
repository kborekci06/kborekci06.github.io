## Redesign of the Northwestern BME Undergraduate Physiology Sequence
**Northwestern University Department of Biomedical Engineering | Physiology Council | February 2026 – Present**


### Project summary

The Northwestern BME department invited me to lead the technical design of a fundamental restructuring of its undergraduate core physiology sequence (BME 304/305/306). The existing three-course sequence is organized by organ system, mirroring how medical schools teach preclinical physiology. However, BME students lack the 500+ hours of anatomy, histology, and clinical reasoning that give medical students context for that content. The result is a sequence that incentivizes memorization of physiological facts over the transferable engineering reasoning that would serve students in capstone, industry, and graduate school.

I authored the initial proposal report to the department's Physiology Council diagnosing this problem, and then led the detailed lecture-level buildout of the first two courses in the redesigned sequence through five major iterative versions.

### My role and contributions

| Phase | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Problem diagnosis** | Authored the initial 8-page proposal identifying the core issue: organ-system organization produces biology-forward courses that don't consistently foreground modeling workflows, computation, or verification/validation as assessable outcomes. |
| **Design philosophy** | Established the "Motifs Over Organs" guiding principle: organizing courses around recurring engineering structures (conservation laws, lumped-parameter networks, feedback/control, distributed models) rather than organ-by-organ coverage. |
| **Sequence architecture** | Proposed a three-course architecture: Course A (systems engineering modeling), Course B (computational neurophysiology and bioelectric systems), Course C (transport, mechanics, and multiphysics in physiology), with cross-course structures including a standardized Model Design Review deliverable and vertical tool fluency progression. |
| **Detailed course buildout** | Designed full 26-lecture plans for KB-1 (Engineering Foundations of Living Systems) and KB-2 (Modeling Physiological Systems), including per-lecture learning objectives, content bullets, source traceability columns, and textbook integration across four primary references. Developed through five major revisions (v1–v5). |
| **Peer benchmarking** | Benchmarked the proposed redesign against six peer institutions (UTSA, Rutgers, Georgia Tech, Cornell, Johns Hopkins, MIT) with 13 references, establishing that the approach aligns with a recognizable national pattern. |
| **Pedagogical innovation** | Developed the "Physiology-First" lecture pattern (dedicated physiology blocks precede each engineering module so students learn the biology first, then formalize it with engineering tools) and a 7-step Physiological System Decomposition Protocol designed as a transferable framework students carry into capstone and industry. |

### Design philosophy
The central insight driving the redesign is that BME students will never retain physiology the way medical students do, because there is no time for spaced repetition of pure physiological fact drilling in an engineering curriculum. The more durable and transferable skill is teaching students how to functionally model a system, and how to pick up a dense physiology text and extract what they need, much like a software engineer consulting library documentation. Every design decision in the course buildout follows from this premise.

### Current status
This project is actively ongoing. I continue to work with the BME department's Physiology Council to refine the course architecture and prepare for implementation. As this work involves internal departmental planning and has not yet been formally adopted, the detailed course buildout documents and proposal materials are not available for public distribution. I am happy to discuss the design philosophy, methodology, and architectural decisions in detail during a conversation.
